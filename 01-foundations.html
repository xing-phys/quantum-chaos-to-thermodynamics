1  States, observables, and equilibrium ensembles

A gas in a sealed box obeys reversible microscopic equations. Nevertheless, its pressure becomes predictable without knowing every particle’s position or every amplitude of its wavefunction. The central question of this book is how a small set of observables can become insensitive to most microscopic information. We begin by defining exactly which information an observable accesses and what an equilibrium prediction means.

1.1 The quantum state as a prediction rule

A normalized vector \(\ket{\psi}\) describes a pure state. The probability of obtaining the result \(a\) when measuring \(A=\sum_a aP_a\) is \(\bra{\psi}P_a\ket{\psi}\). A density matrix packages both pure and mixed preparations:

\[ \rho=\sum_j p_j\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j},\qquad p_j\geq0,\qquad\sum_jp_j=1,\qquad \langle A\rangle_\rho=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A). \tag{1.1}\]

A density matrix is positive, Hermitian, and has unit trace. Its decomposition into preparation vectors is generally not unique; predictions depend on \(\rho\), not on a chosen decomposition. Purity, \(\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2\), equals one for a pure state and is smaller for a mixed state. The von Neumann entropy, in units with \(k_{\mathrm B}=1\), is

\[ S_{\mathrm{vN}}(\rho)=-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho). \tag{1.2}\]

Zero eigenvalues contribute zero because \(x\ln x\to0\) as \(x\to0^+\). Unitary evolution, \(\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U^\dagger(t)\), preserves every eigenvalue of \(\rho\), hence preserves this entropy. An isolated pure state therefore never becomes a mixed Gibbs state as a statement about its full density matrix.

Why, then, can a subsystem become thermal? For \(\mathcal H=\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B\), the reduced state is \(\rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_B\rho\). In bases \(\ket{a}\ket{b}\),

\[ (\rho_A)_{aa'}=\sum_b\rho_{ab,a'b},\qquad \operatorname{Tr}(\rho A\otimes I_B)=\operatorname{Tr}_A(\rho_A A). \tag{1.3}\]

For the entangled vector \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), the global entropy is zero but \(\rho_A=I_A/2\) has entropy \(\ln2\). Entanglement allows local ignorance within a completely specified global state. No averaging over an observer’s ignorance is needed for this example.

1.2 Three ensembles and their assumptions

Suppose energy is the only extensive conserved quantity besides fixed particle number and volume. An isolated equilibrium system with energy known within a window \(\mathcal W=[E-\Delta/2,E+\Delta/2]\) is assigned

\[ \rho_{\mathrm{mc}}=\frac{P_{\mathcal W}}{D_{\mathcal W}},\qquad P_{\mathcal W}=\sum_{E_n\in\mathcal W}\ket n\bra n,\qquad D_{\mathcal W}=\operatorname{Tr}P_{\mathcal W}. \tag{1.4}\]

The window should contain many levels while remaining narrow on the energy scale over which the target observable changes appreciably. The microcanonical prescription is a prediction rule whose dynamical justification remains to be established; it is not an extra term in the Schrödinger equation.

For a system exchanging energy with a large reservoir, the canonical state is

\[ \rho_\beta=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z},\qquad Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H},\qquad\beta=1/T. \tag{1.5}\]

If particles can also be exchanged, \(\rho_{\beta,\mu}=e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}/\mathcal Z\), provided \([H,N]=0\). Other exact charges require corresponding constraints. Throughout this book, comparing states from different conserved-charge sectors without specifying their weights is an avoidable source of confusion.

Here is a derivation of the Boltzmann factor. Let \(H=H_A+H_B+H_{AB}\) and initially neglect the boundary interaction when counting levels. If \(A\) has energy \(\epsilon_a\), the number of compatible reservoir states is \(\Omega_B(E_{\mathrm{tot}}-\epsilon_a)\). Therefore

\[ p_a\propto e^{S_B(E_{\mathrm{tot}}-\epsilon_a)} \simeq e^{S_B(E_{\mathrm{tot}})-\beta\epsilon_a},\qquad \beta=\left.\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial E_B}\right|_{E_{\mathrm{tot}}}. \tag{1.6}\]

The next term is \(-\epsilon_a^2/(2T^2C_B)\) after choosing a suitable reference energy. A reservoir of large heat capacity suppresses this correction. The approximation also requires a coupling weak enough that the subsystem energy remains meaningful. At appreciable coupling, the reduced equilibrium state need not be \(e^{-\beta H_A}/Z_A\) for the bare subsystem Hamiltonian.

The same canonical distribution follows by maximizing \(-\sum_np_n\ln p_n\) with fixed normalization and mean energy. A variation gives \(\ln p_n=-1-\alpha-\beta E_n\). This is a useful inference principle, but maximizing entropy by itself does not prove that a particular Hamiltonian carries an arbitrary preparation to that distribution.

1.3 Thermodynamic functions from a partition function

Differentiating the finite-dimensional partition function gives exact identities:

\[ U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z,\qquad \operatorname{Var}_\beta(H)=\partial_\beta^2\ln Z, \qquad C=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\beta(H)}{T^2}. \tag{1.7}\]

For finite-range systems away from a critical point, both \(U\) and \(C\) usually scale as the number of sites \(L\). Thus the energy width scales as \(\sqrt L\) while the mean energy is extensive. The width in energy density, \(\Delta e=\Delta E/L\), vanishes as \(L^{-1/2}\). This is the scale separation behind much ensemble equivalence.

More explicitly, write the number of states near \(E=Le\) as \(\exp[Ls(e)+o(L)]\). The canonical sum is approximated by

\[ Z(\beta)\approx\int \mathrm de\,\exp\{L[s(e)-\beta e]\}. \tag{1.8}\]

If \(s(e)\) is smooth and strictly concave near a unique maximum of \(s(e)-\beta e\), a saddle point gives \(s'(e_*)=\beta\) and a narrow distribution around \(e_*\). A local observable smooth in \(e\) then has the same leading prediction in microcanonical and canonical ensembles. This argument assumes the thermodynamic limit and thermodynamic stability. Long-range interactions, first-order coexistence, and nonconcave entropy can obstruct simple versions of equivalence. Even when local observables agree, the global density matrices and energy fluctuations can differ greatly.

For \(L\) independent two-level systems with energies \(0\) and \(\epsilon\), \(Z=(1+e^{-\beta\epsilon})^L\) and the excited fraction is \(q=(1+e^{\beta\epsilon})^{-1}\). The microcanonical count with \(M=Lq\) excitations is \(\binom LM\). Stirling’s formula gives \(s(q)=-q\ln q-(1-q)\ln(1-q)\), so \(\partial S/\partial E=\epsilon^{-1}\ln[(1-q)/q]\), recovering the same temperature. This solvable counting problem illustrates ensemble equivalence without assuming chaos.

1.4 Typicality: a kinematic fact with a dynamical limitation

Let a constrained subspace \(\mathcal H_R\) have dimension \(D_R\) and define \(\Omega=P_R/D_R\). Choose a unit vector uniformly with the unitary invariant Haar measure in this subspace. For an observable \(A_R=P_RAP_R\),

\[ \mathbb E\langle A\rangle_\psi=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}_R A_R}{D_R},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\langle A\rangle_\psi)= \frac{\operatorname{Tr}_R(A_R^2)/D_R-[\operatorname{Tr}_R A_R/D_R]^2}{D_R+1}. \tag{1.9}\]

One obtains this by using \(\mathbb E[(\ket\psi\bra\psi)^{\otimes2}]=(I+\mathsf F)/[D_R(D_R+1)]\), where \(\mathsf F\) swaps two copies of the subspace. The variance is at most \(\|A\|^2/(D_R+1)\). Most vectors therefore give nearly the same answer for any fixed bounded observable when \(D_R\) is large. The canonical-typicality results of  [1] establish corresponding closeness statements for a sufficiently small subsystem under appropriate dimension conditions; see also  [2] for the energy-shell formulation.

This is a statement about measure on a space of vectors. A time-independent Hamiltonian keeps the absolute values of its energy-basis coefficients fixed, so a physical orbit never samples all Haar-random vectors. Moreover, easily prepared low-entanglement states are atypical. Typicality explains why thermal-looking pure states are abundant; it leaves open whether physical dynamics reaches them, and how quickly. Those questions motivate quantum chaos and ETH.

NoteWhat does “thermal” mean here?

Specify an observable class and an accuracy. A subsystem is thermal when its reduced state is close enough to an equilibrium state to reproduce that class. Equilibration means small deviations from a stationary value for most times. Thermalization additionally identifies that value with an equilibrium ensemble fixed by the conserved macroscopic data.

1.5 Exercises and worked answers

1. Entropy without mixing. For \(\ket\psi=\sqrt p\ket{00}+\sqrt{1-p}\ket{11}\), calculate the purity and entropy of \(A\).

TipAnswer

Tracing \(B\) removes the cross terms: \(\rho_A=\operatorname{diag}(p,1-p)\). Therefore \(\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A^2=p^2+(1-p)^2\) and \(S_A=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p)\). The global state remains pure for every \(p\).

2. Finite reservoir correction. Show that \(S_B''(E_B)=-1/(T^2C_B)\) and identify when the quadratic correction in Equation 1.6 matters.

TipAnswer

Differentiate \(S_B'=1/T\): \(S_B''=-(1/T^2)(\partial T/\partial E_B)=-1/(T^2C_B)\). Expanding around the mean subsystem energy gives a correction of order \((\epsilon_a-\bar\epsilon)^2/(T^2C_B)\). It is small when the reservoir heat capacity greatly exceeds the subsystem energy-variance scale measured in units of \(T^2\).

3. A zero-temperature caveat. Why does the saddle-point argument not automatically justify a canonical description of an arbitrary low-energy pure state?

TipAnswer

It compares equilibrium ensembles under smoothness and large-deviation assumptions. It says nothing about a pure state’s overlaps with eigenstates. Near a spectral edge, the number of participating levels may be small and the entropy expansion can fail. Dynamical thermalization requires additional information about the eigenstates and the preparation.
